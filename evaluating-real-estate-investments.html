Garrett O'Rourke on Evaluating Real Estate Investments

📅 October 5, 2026
✎ Garrett O'Rourke
⌚ 3 min read

Garrett O'Rourke approaches real estate the way he approaches any business decision: numbers first, story second. Property has a reputation as a safe bet, but he believes a building is only a good investment if the purchase makes sense on its own terms.

O'Rourke, a Miami Beach-based business executive and private investor who serves as President of Commercial Development Group, invests primarily in stocks and real estate. His perspective comes from running businesses rather than from a finance credential, a background summarized on Garrett’s about.me profile. What follows is personal philosophy, shaped by his path from sales to investing, not investment advice.

Run the Numbers Before the Narrative

Every property comes with a story: an up-and-coming neighborhood, a rising market, a seller with a great pitch. O'Rourke's background makes him wary of persuasive stories. Years in sales taught him to separate genuine value from good presentation, and that skill matters just as much when he is the one being sold to.

So he starts with the fundamentals: purchase price, financing, carrying costs, cash flow, maintenance, taxes and insurance. Only after those numbers work does he consider appreciation potential. Real estate is not automatically a good investment simply because property has historically gone up in value.

A property doesn't become a good investment because the market has been rising. It becomes one because the numbers work.

Hidden Costs and Downside Scenarios

In O'Rourke's view, the costs investors forget are often the ones that decide the outcome: vacancies, repairs, insurance increases, rising taxes and the time it takes to manage a property well. In South Florida in particular, insurance and maintenance can change the economics of a deal faster than many buyers expect.

That is why he looks at the downside before the upside. What happens if rents soften, rates stay high or a major repair arrives early? An investment that only works when everything goes right is a fragile one. It is the same owner's mindset he developed while operating call centers, where every seat carried a cost and every lead had been paid for.

Negotiation and the Willingness to Walk Away

Real estate rewards patience, information and negotiation, and Garrett sees all three as sales skills. Good negotiators listen more than they talk, understand what the other side actually needs and know their own limits before the conversation begins.

Most importantly, he believes the willingness to walk away is one of an investor's strongest advantages. Not every opportunity deserves pursuit, and sometimes the best investment decision is the deal you decline. Excitement is not a strategy; discipline is.

Conclusion

For O'Rourke, real estate is a business like any other. Location, price and cash flow still matter more than enthusiasm, and patience is often worth more than speed. Treat each property as an operating business, respect the risks and let the numbers lead.
